Working research framework · November 2026

Why this research

Technology is never only a tool. Its rules, interfaces, incentives, data practices and operating arrangements distribute choices and consequences. They influence who can participate, whose knowledge is heard, what work is visible, how conflict is handled and which outcomes an organization rewards.

Organizations often measure efficiency, growth, engagement and output because those measures are comparatively easy to count. Those measures are useful, but they are incomplete. When they become the whole definition of success, people can be reduced to users to retain, workers to measure, audiences to monetize or data to classify.

This project asks what leadership is responsible for when technology becomes part of the environment in which people work, learn, play and relate to one another. Games provide a practical setting in which rules, power, incentives, cooperation, exclusion and recovery can be examined through designed choices and observed play.

Research question

How do organizational leadership practices shape the design and use of technology, and how can creative systems—particularly games—be designed and governed to support human agency, dignity, cooperation and social well-being?

Questions within the study

  1. Leadership: What assumptions about people, power, productivity and responsibility shape technology decisions?
  2. Design: How do those assumptions become embedded in rules, interfaces, algorithms, rewards, workflows and data practices?
  3. Experience: How do participants experience agency, fairness, belonging, trust, frustration and opportunity?
  4. Social behavior: What kinds of competition, cooperation, domination, restraint, reciprocity and repair do designed systems make possible or reward?
  5. Organization: What governance, labor, accessibility and decision-making practices help a creative technology organization sustain both its work and the people doing it?
  6. Transfer: Which lessons are specific to one project, and which can be responsibly applied in other organizations?

Structural inequity, leadership and intergenerational opportunity

Leadership determines who has access to education, work, resources, decision-making and institutional accountability. Racial disparities in these pathways can accumulate across generations through policy, funding, eligibility rules and unequal access to mentorship. This is a research question about systems, not a deficit attributed to individuals.

Historical policy case: Minnesota Family Investment Program (MFIP)

MDRC's adult evaluation reports random assignment of more than 14,000 families between April 1994 and March 1996. Its six-year follow-up examines parents and children and reports persistent economic effects for some disadvantaged single-parent groups. These findings justify examining how policy design shapes family opportunities; they do not establish that MFIP caused later racial leadership disparities.

A historical institutional-records inquiry initiated in September 2026 seeks research approvals, consent and notification procedures, randomization, data governance, complaints, aggregate group counts and archival disposition. The recovered correspondence through October 2026 includes agency referrals and a state records-request closure citing records no longer held. The underlying approvals and disposition remain unverified. Missing records are not proof of misconduct. Private family records and correspondence are excluded from public documentation.

Doctoral pathways and historically Black colleges and universities

NCSES Survey of Earned Doctorates reports that Black U.S. citizen and permanent resident science and engineering doctorate recipients increased from 812 (5%) in 2004 to 1,754 (6%) in 2024. NCSES baccalaureate-origin research reports that 25.7% of Black doctorate recipients with U.S. bachelor's degrees in 2010–2020 earned those degrees at HBCUs. These are attainment and origin statistics, not completion rates or direct measures of leadership representation.

Examine direct leadership representation, doctoral completion and attrition, funding, time to degree, mentorship, accessibility and documented corrective action separately. Compare institutions using consistent denominators, years and populations; mark unavailable outcomes as unverified. Test whether racial and disability inequities compound rather than assume causation.

Research position

The study treats technology as a sociotechnical arrangement: people, tools, organizational structures and working practices affect one another. It does not assume that a humane intention produces a humane outcome. The question is whether the design and the organization make that outcome more likely, and what evidence supports the claim.

It also distinguishes two different uses of games. Games can be studied as designed experiences in their own right, and they can be used as bounded experimental environments for examining decisions and interaction. Neither use makes gameplay a direct prediction of how a person will behave in society. Findings must remain tied to the context, participants and method that produced them.

A central distinction is between designing to steer people toward an organizational target and designing conditions in which people can exercise meaningful choice, develop capability and contribute to shared outcomes. This is a research tension, not a conclusion to impose on participants.

What will be examined

  • Human agency: meaningful choices, understandable consequences and the ability to question or refuse a system.
  • Dignity and safety: how mistakes, disagreement, privacy, boundaries and exit are handled.
  • Fairness: how rules distribute opportunity, burdens, information and power.
  • Access and participation: whether different abilities, devices, communication styles and levels of experience can take part.
  • Belonging and trust: whether people are heard, represented accurately and treated consistently.
  • Cooperation and repair: whether systems support mutual benefit, accountability, recovery and changed behavior after conflict.
  • Work and recognition: whether contributions, learning, authorship, compensation and decision rights are made clear.

These are proposed areas of observation, not a validated measurement scale. Instruments and definitions will need review, piloting and, where appropriate, participant consultation.

Practice-research setting

Loptr Lab is the primary practice setting: a founder-led creative technology studio whose projects make different organizational and design questions observable. Project boundaries remain intact; a shared research framework does not merge their mechanics, narrative worlds, intellectual property or release authority.

  • Studio practice: leadership decisions, production structures, resource allocation, collaboration, authorship, governance and organizational learning.
  • Veiled Dominion: asymmetric power, strategic responsibility, restraint, sacrifice and the consequences of competing objectives.
  • Duet: rules implementation, accessibility, fairness, recovery, player understanding and consistency across play environments.
  • PIXIE and related narrative work: authorship, attribution, memory, cultural representation and the distribution of creative agency.
  • Training and contributor pathways: learning through real work, recognition, safe participation, access boundaries and the difference between practice and employment.

Each project remains governed by its own repository, rules authority, canon, rights and release process. Research notes do not change project rules or authorize creative work.

Methods and evidence

The proposed design is longitudinal and practice-based, combining qualitative and quantitative evidence where each is appropriate.

  1. Document the organization: maintain a dated record of decisions, roles, constraints, changes and reasons. Separate contemporaneous records from later recollection.
  2. Analyze design artifacts: examine rules, interfaces, workflows, reward structures, accessibility decisions, governance documents and version history.
  3. Test the implementation: verify whether software behaves as the documented rules and design intend. A passing automated test is evidence of a tested condition, not proof of a good player experience.
  4. Conduct playtests: observe first-use understanding, decision paths, collaboration, breakdowns, recovery and participant interpretation. Record context and avoid coaching unless the protocol calls for it.
  5. Gather participant perspectives: use interviews, questionnaires or facilitated discussion only under an approved protocol with informed consent and appropriate safeguards.
  6. Compare cases: examine other independent creative technology organizations to distinguish local experience from patterns that may transfer.
  7. Analyze cautiously: compare stated intentions, implemented behavior, observed experience and organizational conditions. Report uncertainty, negative results and competing explanations.

Small exploratory groups can identify usability and interpretation problems; they cannot establish population-wide prevalence. Larger or comparative studies require a defined outcome, sampling plan and justified sample-size calculation. No fixed participant count is treated as universally sufficient.

Participant protection and research ethics

  • Research participation is voluntary and separate from employment, training access, contributor status, payment, game access or community standing.
  • Do not treat routine product feedback as research consent. Obtain informed consent before collecting or reusing participant data for research.
  • Secure institutional research ethics approval before recruitment or data collection that requires it. Do not describe proposed interviews or playtests as completed research.
  • Collect only the information needed for the stated purpose. Set retention, access, anonymization and deletion rules before collection.
  • Make withdrawal, breaks and stopping participation practical. Do not shame participants, equate performance with personal worth or infer a diagnosis from play.
  • Provide accessible participation routes and explain any limitations honestly.
  • Separate researcher interpretation from participant statements and preserve dissenting or contradictory evidence.

Working principle: We test the system, not the worth of the person using it.

How the work will be judged

Three questions must remain distinct:

  • Design: are the intended rules, values and participant protections clearly stated and open to scrutiny?
  • Technical: does the implementation behave as specified across the supported environments?
  • Human experience: can participants understand, access and meaningfully engage with the experience, and how do they describe its effects?

Expert review, software verification and player experience are different forms of evidence. None substitutes for the others. Positive feedback from a small or self-selected group is not proof of broad social benefit.

Literature starting points

  1. Ang, K. C. S., Sankaran, S. & Liu, D. (2025). Advancing sociotechnical systems theory: New principles for human-robot team design and development. Applied Ergonomics, 129, 104604. doi:10.1016/j.apergo.2025.104604. A recent framework connecting human-centered design, participation, organizational alignment, trust and reliability.
  2. Deterding, S. (2019). Gamification in Management: Between Choice Architecture and Humanistic Design. Journal of Management Inquiry, 28(2), 131–136. doi:10.1177/1056492618790912. A useful distinction between behavior steering through incentives and design centered on meaningful human experience.
  3. Grace, L. (2024). The Patterns of Games for Leading Social Change. Journal of Leadership Studies, 17(4), 56–63. doi:10.1002/jls.21880. Examines play, iterative problem-solving and games in leadership for social impact.
  4. van Dijk, E. & De Dreu, C. K. W. (2021). Experimental Games and Social Decision Making. Annual Review of Psychology, 72, 415–438. doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-081420-110718. Reviews experimental games as methods for studying cooperation, coordination, conflict and social norms.

This is a starting bibliography, not a completed literature review. The next review should include organizational leadership, humanistic management, disability and accessibility studies, participatory design, labor and platform governance, research ethics, and critical game studies.
